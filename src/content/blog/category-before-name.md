The hardest part of building Kigumi has not been the engineering. It has been the sentence.

When you build inside an existing category, the first sentence is free. "It's a CRM, but for X." "It's observability for Y." The listener already has a shelf in their head. You hand them the thing, they put it on the shelf, and the conversation moves on to whether yours is better. The category does the explaining for you.

There is no shelf for this. Every conversation starts by building one.

## What that actually feels like

I have had the same fifteen minutes a hundred times. A founder or a CTO describes a pain. AI is writing more of their code. Nobody on the team can fully say what the system does anymore. Onboarding takes longer than it used to. A change in one place breaks something nobody predicted, because nobody could predict it, because nobody holds the whole behavior in their head and it is written down nowhere.

They describe the problem with precision. Then I ask what they call it, and there is a pause. They do not have a word for it. They have been living inside it for months and it has no name.

That pause is the entire company. It is also the hardest thing to sell, because a problem without a name is a problem people have learned to route around rather than solve. They have built habits on top of it. The senior engineer who is the only one who understands the auth flow is not seen as a risk. He is seen as load-bearing. The thing is invisible because it has always been there.

## The two failure modes

When you are early in a category that does not exist yet, there are two ways to lose, and they pull in opposite directions.

The first is to name it too aggressively. Declare the category, plant the flag, write the manifesto. It reads as arrogance when there is no product behind it yet, and it invites every adjacent vendor to claim the word before you have earned it. I have watched companies name a category in a launch post and spend the next year being the second-best example of it.

The second is to never name it at all. Describe only the pain, stay safe, let the customer keep their own words for it. This feels humble and is actually fatal. If you never give the problem a name, someone else does, and the name they choose decides how the whole market thinks about it, including how it thinks about you.

I do not have this resolved. The honest state is that the internal name and the external name are not the same word yet, on purpose, because saying the largest version of the claim out loud before it is earned is the first failure mode and I would rather make the second mistake than that one. That is a bet, not a certainty. I am telling you it is a bet because the alternative is to pretend I know, and I do not.

## What carries you through it

The thing that keeps this from being demoralizing is narrow and specific: the pause is consistent. Different companies, different stacks, different stages, same fifteen minutes, same pause at the same question. When the shape of a problem repeats that exactly across people who have never spoken to each other, the problem is real even though the word is missing. The word being missing is the opportunity, not the absence of one.

Observability was a set of disconnected pains before it was a category. People shipped logs, metrics, and traces as three separate habits and did not have one word that held them. Feature management was "we have some if-statements and an admin toggle" before it was a discipline anyone budgeted for. Someone had the same fifteen-minute conversation, over and over, before either of those had a shelf.

I do not get to know in advance whether the name I am circling is the one that sticks. No founder in this position does. What I get to know is whether the pause is real, and it is. The category is not the thing you declare. It is the thing that already exists in a hundred people's frustration, waiting for the sentence that lets them point at it.

Most days the work is finding that sentence and being wrong, and coming back the next day to be wrong more precisely. That is slower than writing code and it does not show up on any chart. It is still the actual job.
